Designing a control room (SOC/NOC)

What separates a control room that works from a wall of screens: source distribution, KVM, operator ergonomics and the room services that keep it running 24/7.

A control room is where an organisation watches its most critical operations around the clock. A Security Operations Centre (SOC) monitors cameras, access control and alarms; a Network Operations Centre (NOC) watches IT infrastructure, links and applications; many facilities combine both. Whatever the mission, the design challenge is the same: present huge amounts of information clearly, let operators act on it fast, and keep running 24/7 without a stumble. Getting there is a discipline that spans audiovisual engineering, ergonomics and building services.

The video wall and source distribution

The video wall is the shared picture the whole team works from. Fine-pitch LED or narrow-bezel LCD tiles are chosen for viewing distance, brightness and uptime, then driven by a processor that can place any source — a camera feed, a dashboard, a map, a spreadsheet — as a window anywhere on the canvas. Behind it, an AV-over-IP distribution layer carries sources across the network so any signal can reach any display or operator without a fixed matrix. A KVM system extends that to control: operators drive machines in the equipment room from their desks, keeping heat and noise out of the operating floor while switching instantly between systems.

Operators and ergonomics

Technology is only useful if people can sustain attention at it for a full shift. Consoles are specified for continuous 24/7 use, with sit-stand adjustment, proper sightlines to the video wall, cable management and monitor arms positioned to avoid neck and eye strain. Sightline geometry decides how far consoles sit from the wall and how the room tiers. Shift patterns, break spaces and clear circulation matter too — an exhausted operator misses events, however good the displays. Standardised console layouts speed shift handover, because an incoming operator finds every control exactly where the last shift left it, and enough operator positions with sensible failover keep the floor staffed even when a seat is offline.

Acoustics, lighting and reliability

The room itself is engineered. Acoustic treatment controls reverberation so alarms and voice communication stay intelligible. Lighting is dimmable and glare-free, kept low enough to read screens but bright enough to stay alert, ideally tunable across a shift. Reliability is designed in: redundant power and UPS, cooling sized for a dense equipment room, and networks with no single point of failure, so a hardware fault never blinds the operators. Documented commissioning proves each of these before the room goes live.

Frequently asked

What's the difference between a SOC and a NOC?

A SOC focuses on physical and cyber security — surveillance, access control and threat monitoring. A NOC focuses on IT and network health — availability, performance and incidents. Many organisations run a combined room sharing the same video wall and infrastructure.

Why use AV-over-IP and KVM instead of a traditional matrix?

An IP-based distribution layer lets any source reach any display or operator over the network, scales without re-cabling, and — with KVM — keeps noisy, hot computers in the equipment room while operators control them from the floor.

What makes a control room reliable enough for 24/7 use?

Redundant power and UPS, cooling sized for the load, networks with no single point of failure, consoles rated for continuous use, and documented commissioning that proves each system before go-live.
