Smart buildings: converging ELV systems for Vision 2030

How network, security, AV, BMS and lighting are collapsing onto one IP backbone — and why that changes how buildings are designed, run and future-proofed.

For decades, a building's low-voltage systems grew up in isolation. The network team pulled data cabling, the security contractor ran its own coax and controllers, the AV vendor installed proprietary matrix switchers, and the BMS supplier wired sensors on a separate bus. Each subsystem worked, but none of them talked to the others. The smart building reverses that logic: it treats structured cabling and the IP network as a shared utility that every discipline rides on, from access control and CCTV to audiovisual distribution, HVAC, lighting and fire detection.

The shift to a converged IP backbone

Convergence is possible because almost every ELV device now speaks IP. Cameras stream over the network, door controllers sit on PoE switches, AV signals travel as AV-over-IP flows, lighting gateways expose BACnet/IP, and building management platforms pull telemetry over the same fabric. Consolidating these onto one structured cabling system designed to TIA-568 and ISO/IEC 11801 cuts duplicated infrastructure, simplifies pathways and containment, and gives facilities a single place to see what the building is doing. It also means capacity, redundancy and cybersecurity can be planned once, holistically, rather than patched into many parallel networks.

Where convergence pays off

The benefits are operational as much as technical. A converged building lets security, safety and comfort systems share data: an alarm can trigger cameras, unlock egress routes, and push a message to digital signage in one automated sequence. Energy management improves when lighting, HVAC and occupancy sensing feed a common analytics layer. Maintenance gets easier because faults surface on one dashboard instead of a separate portal per vendor. And future changes — a new tenant fit-out, an added floor, a signage rollout — become configuration tasks on an existing backbone rather than fresh cabling projects. Fire detection remains a special case: EN 54 and SBC 801 govern its design, and Civil Defense approval is mandatory, so life-safety keeps its integrity even while it integrates.

Why one integrator matters

Convergence only delivers when someone owns the whole picture. When separate contractors design in isolation, the seams between systems become the weak points — mismatched VLANs, undocumented dependencies, and finger-pointing when something breaks. A single integrator designs the cabling, network, security, AV and BMS layers to one standard, tests the interfaces between them, and hands over documentation that reflects the building as built. That is the model behind Vision 2030's giga-projects and smart cities, where thousands of devices must behave as one coordinated system from day one.

Frequently asked

What counts as an ELV system?

Extra-low-voltage systems are the building's data and signal networks — structured cabling, CCTV, access control, AV, public address, IPTV, fire detection and the BMS — as opposed to high-voltage power. Convergence brings these onto a shared IP backbone.

Does converging systems make fire detection less safe?

No. Fire alarm design still follows EN 54 and SBC 801 and requires Civil Defense approval. It can share network infrastructure for monitoring while keeping its own certified detection and alarm paths intact.

Can we converge an existing building or only new ones?

Both. Retrofits usually migrate one subsystem at a time onto a common backbone, starting with structured cabling, so you gain convergence in stages without disrupting operations.
